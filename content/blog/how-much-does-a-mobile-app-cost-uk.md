The price of a mobile app depends mostly on what it has to do. A simple app with a handful of screens costs a fraction of an app with accounts, payments and a custom backend. Here is what moves the price, what to budget for after launch, and what we charge.

## What drives the price

- **Screens and features.** More screens and more behaviour mean more build and testing time. This is the biggest factor.
- **Accounts, payments and notifications.** Each one adds integration work and testing.
- **A backend.** If the app stores data or has user accounts, something has to run behind it. A backend typically adds {{addon:extra_backend}} to a project.
- **Design.** If you already have designs, the build is quicker. If you only have sketches, basic interface design adds {{addon:extra_design}}.
- **Platforms.** Android only is the simplest. Supporting iOS as well adds work and is scoped per project.
- **Release.** Preparing the store listing, screenshots and release builds takes time. The stores also charge for developer accounts: Google Play has a one-time fee of US$25, and the Apple Developer Program costs US$99 a year.

## Who builds it changes the price too

- **Agencies** bring a team of designers, developers and project managers. That suits large projects, but the overhead is part of every quote, so even a first version often runs into five figures.
- **Freelancers** are usually the cheapest option per hour, but you take on more of the coordination yourself, and continuity depends on one person's availability.
- **Small companies** sit between the two: one accountable point of contact, written scope and invoices from a registered business, without the overhead of a large team.

Whoever you choose, compare quotes on the same written scope. "Simple app" and "MVP" mean very different things to different people.

## Costs after launch

The build is not the only cost. Plan for:

- **Hosting and services.** A backend, database or services such as Firebase have monthly costs, often small at first and growing with your users.
- **Updates.** Android and iOS release new versions every year, and the stores raise their minimum requirements. An app that is never updated eventually stops being accepted for new releases.
- **Fixes and small improvements.** Real users find issues and ask for changes. Setting aside a small monthly or quarterly budget is easier than handling it as an emergency.

## Ways to keep the cost down

- Start with one platform and add the second later.
- Keep the first version to what your users actually need.
- Bring sketches or designs, even rough ones.
- Use a ready-made service such as Firebase instead of a custom backend where it fits.
- Agree a small first milestone and review it before the next one.
